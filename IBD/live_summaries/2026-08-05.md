### 6. Trading Education & Process
- **Progressive Exposure Is the Antidote to Whiplash:**
    Ali Coram demonstrated the core strategy for this market: scaling into exposure incrementally as signals appear, rather than waiting for a single "go" moment. She started with S&P 500 leveraged ETFs on Friday's retake of the 50-day, switched to faster horses on Monday, and added TQQQ on the Nasdaq breakout. Webby explicitly endorsed this: "99% of the folks should just follow what Ali's doing." This builds a position cushion and avoids the paralysis of buying everything on one high-risk day.

- **The "Expectation Breaker" as a Defining Moment:**
    Webby highlighted Friday's action on the Nasdaq as a classic expectation breaker. After a brutal sell-off post-Fed, the logical expectation was for a gap down to the 200-day line. Instead, the market gapped up, never looked back, and closed strong. This is the opposite of the October 2007 expectation breaker that preceded a bear market. Recognizing these moments—when the market violently flips the script—is key to catching major turns.

- **The Low of the Follow-Through Day Is Your Line in the Sand:**
    Webby introduced a core MarketSchool rule: the low of the follow-through day becomes the ultimate stop-loss. It's a simple, unambiguous level that doesn't rely on the flaws of volume-based distribution counting. A close below that low nullifies the rally attempt and forces a return to defense. Until then, intraday noise should be largely ignored.

- **Know When to Walk Away from an Options Trade:**
    Harold Morris provided a masterclass in discipline. His SPY bull put spread was set up, but the underlying was gapping and whipping around, driving the short strike in and out of the money. Instead of forcing the trade to "get filled," he stepped back. "I just don't like what I see here, so I'm not going to execute the trade." This is the practical application of his mantra: "I like to get filled; I don't have to get filled."

- **Quick Death vs. Slow Death on Earnings:**
    CVS and EXTR were the textbook examples of post-earnings action where you must act fast. Harold's rule: if a stock gaps down on earnings, go to the 5-minute chart. If you get a tiny bounce, sell into it. If you hesitate, you're stuck at the lows. In contrast, LLY was a "slow bleed" from the open, which can be even more dangerous for a conviction stock if you don't have a clear plan.
