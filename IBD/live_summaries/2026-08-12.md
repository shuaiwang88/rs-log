### 6. Trading Education & Process
- **Contextualizing Wide & Loose Charts:**
    Webby used Dell and Schwab (1998) to illustrate that a chart can look "junk" in isolation but still be a leader when compared to its sector or the broader market. Dell's wide, loose action is bad, but its positive divergence against AI peers (SNDK, MU) and the historical high-tight flag context gives it grace.

- **The Gap-Up Fade vs. Build:**
    On days with a gap-up, Webby uses the 50% retracement level between yesterday's close and today's high as the line. If price stays above that, the gap-up is intact; if it undercuts, the thesis weakens. The decision to add exposure is triggered by taking out the morning high, not by buying the initial pop.

- **Why Not Buy Stops?**
    Webby explained that revealing exact stop levels on a product like SwingTrader would allow large players to "push stocks around" to trigger stops. This is like playing poker with cards face-up. Individual traders can use buy stops, but the product management must avoid publishing precise levels.

- **The Art of the Wedge:**
    Ken and Webby debated the BE chart. A true wedge is a slow, tight drift up without shakeouts. If there are upside reversals and red days that shake out weak holders, it's less of a wedge and more of a normal base. Chart reading is an art, not a science.

- **Don't Short Tech Near New Highs:**
    Webby emphatically repeated Bill O'Neil's rule: don't short strong stocks near new highs in a bull market. Shorting is for broken charts, head-and-shoulders tops, and late-stage base failures—not for leaders making new highs.

- **The Last-Hour Trading Philosophy:**
    Ali and Webby discussed the value of making most trading decisions in the last 30 minutes of the day. This provides the most evidence and reduces the need to monitor intraday action. The trade-off is paying up for stocks that have moved, but for busy investors, it's a better balance.
