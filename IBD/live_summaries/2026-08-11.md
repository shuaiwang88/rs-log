### 6. Trading Education & Process
- **The 50-Day Rule: Life Above and Below the Line:**
    David Ryan drew a clear line in the sand: avoid any stock with a declining or flattening 50-day moving average that has had a huge run. These stocks (NBIS, Dell, etc.) have trapped sellers and offer poor risk/reward for longs. Conversely, stocks that have pulled back, reclaimed the 50-day, and are now sitting quietly (LFUS, CSCO) are the ones to watch for the next move.

- **The Undercut-and-Rally: Spotting Institutional Accumulation:**
    David Ryan highlighted a classic bottoming pattern: a stock undercuts a long-term low, then immediately strings together 5-9 consecutive up days. This signals that an institution is aggressively accumulating shares at the point of maximum bearishness. He used **OLLI** and **ELF** as textbook examples and initiated a small position in OLLI based on this logic.

- **"Don't Cheat Just Below a Buy Point":**
    Ed Carson reiterated a critical risk-management rule, specifically for the many AI/semi stocks testing their 50-day lines. The potential for a short-seller to reject the stock right at that line is just as high as the potential for a breakout. The correct approach is to wait for a **decisive** move above resistance, not to try to buy in anticipation of it.

- **Use Weekly Charts to Filter Out the Noise:**
    Chris Gessel explained his weekend screening process: he looks at weekly charts first. Most stocks that pop up on daily scans are still going sideways on a weekly or monthly timeframe. He is looking for clear character changes, like in **AVT**, where a long-term sideways stock suddenly has a powerful breakout with an RS line at new highs. This is a signal that the story has fundamentally changed.

- **The "Wide and Loose" Warning:**
    David Ryan described **Dell** as a "wide and loose sloppy base." 100-point swings, big reversals, and an inability to hold breakouts are classic signs of distribution, not accumulation. A stock should be quiet, tight, and drifting before a breakout. When it's wide and loose, it's either an avoid or a short.
