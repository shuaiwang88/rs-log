### 6. Trading Education & Process (Psychology & Strategy)
- **The "Source of Funds" Method for Market Turns:**
    Mike Webster reiterated his strategy for getting invested during a market inflection point. Because individual stocks are often too extended or don't offer a perfect entry right at the turn, he buys broad-based ETFs (SPY, QQQ, IWM) heavily. Then, as individual names set up over the following days and weeks, he trims the ETFs and uses that capital to buy the stocks. Ali Coram has successfully executed this publicly.

- **When a Great Short Setup Fails (The Palantir Playbook):**
    Webby explained that a stock which looks like a perfect short (like Palantir, which had been a dog for years) but refuses to break down on earnings can become a powerful long. When the stock cleared the 200-day line on massive volume and the shorts couldn't push it back down on the subsequent low-volume pullback, it signaled a short-squeeze and a major character change. "The best-looking shorts that don't end up working out can turn into your best longs."

- **The "Costanza" Stock and Handling Volatility:**
    Scott St. Clair described Dell as the "George Costanza" stock—the opposite of whatever you think it will do. To trade a high-ATR, whip-prone name like Dell, you must either size small enough to withstand a 50-day test or use very tight stops and be willing to re-enter multiple times. Ken's strategy of taking a tiny quarter position on the Friday upside reversal with a clear stop is the template.

- **Respecting the "No Progress" Feedback:**
    Scott gave an honest assessment of his own trading. Despite the indexes looking great, his portfolio has been a carousel of one good stock versus one bad stock, resulting in no progress. His rule: if you're not making money with 20% of your account, you won't make money with 40%. He is using this feedback to stay small and not force exposure.

- **The Pullback Scare and Dollar-Cost Averaging Into Strength:**
    Webby noted that in a new uptrend, pullbacks always look scary and tempt you to wait for a better price. But the best stocks often don't give you that perfect gentle dip. He suggested a "baby dollar-cost averaging" approach: decide on a target exposure by week's end and buy incrementally each day, perhaps using a TWAP order, to ensure you get on board without trying to time the perfect intraday entry.
